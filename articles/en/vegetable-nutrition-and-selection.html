Vegetable Nutrition

At least 300 g daily, with more than half dark-coloured. Combine fibre, vitamin C, and calcium sources, then evaluate realistic portions.

Four conclusions for vegetable selection

MeasureReferenceBasis
Vegetables≥300 g / dayEdible raw weight
Dark-coloured vegetables>½Of vegetable intake
Variety3–5 / dayDistinct ingredients
Dietary fibre25–30 g / dayWhole diet, not vegetables alone

Combine dark leafy greens with broccoli or peppers and watery vegetables. Count edamame as a legume and energy source. Compare nutrients per 100 g, plan intake per serving, and obtain fibre across the whole diet. [1], [10]

Choose by nutritional role

RoleCommon optionsPortions and conditions
FibreEdamame, broccoli; whole grains and legumes elsewhere in the diet100 g prepared shelled edamame contains about 5.2 g total dietary fibre; pods are excluded. [3]
Vitamin CRed or green sweet peppers, broccoliNIH lists about 95 mg in half a cup of raw red pepper and 60 mg in green pepper. Variety and preparation affect values. [6]
CalciumBok choy and other low-oxalate greens, including kaleThese contribute dietary calcium. Spinach calcium is poorly absorbed; dairy and fortified foods provide additional sources. [5]
Provitamin ACarrots, dark leafy greensBeta-carotene requires conversion; its mass is not equivalent to the same mass of vitamin A. [7]
Low-energy food volumeCucumber, winter melon, lettuceUseful alongside other vegetables. Lower content of selected nutrients does not mean no nutritional value. Oil and sauces affect meal energy.
Flavour and varietyCilantro, shepherd’s purse, watercressOptional rotation choices. A small herb garnish is not a 100 g serving.

Serving contribution = amount per 100 g × edible portion ÷ 100. 200 g raw broccoli plus 100 g prepared edamame supplies about 10.4 g fibre, only part of the daily reference range. Raw and prepared entries support meal estimates, not conclusions about the effect of cooking.

What nutrient rankings can establish

A 2014 study scored 47 fruits and vegetables using 17 nutrients per 100 kcal; watercress scored 100. Scores were capped and omitted phytochemical constituents. This is neither a complete nutritional assessment nor a clinical ranking of health outcomes. [4]

In the same study, leaf lettuce scored 70.73, romaine 63.48, and broccoli 34.89. These results contradict a blanket claim that lettuce ranks last, while establishing no superiority for every individual nutrient. Realistic serving size still determines intake.

The supplied text gives no traceable study, indicator weights, or sampling frame for its separate claim that shepherd’s purse and cilantro rank first and second among 38 vegetables. That ranking is not used as a selection criterion here.

Fractions from NIH; not equal food weights or an individual prediction. [5]

ClaimRequired qualification
More calcium than milk means a better calcium sourceContent, serving size, and absorption all matter. NIH cites approximately 5% absorption for spinach and 27% for milk; these are not universal constants. Low-oxalate greens can provide usable calcium without being nutritionally equivalent to a serving of milk. [5]
One pepper always supplies a full day of vitamin CSize, edible yield, and cultivar vary. Storage and heating can reduce vitamin C; edible portions are a more precise basis than the number of peppers. [6]
2,590 μg carotene establishes a fixed vitamin A percentageThe carotenoid and equivalent unit must be specified. Food beta-carotene uses 6 μg per 1 μg RE or 12 μg per 1 μg RAE. Reference-intake units must match; the stated 33% cannot be checked from that number alone. [7], [12]
A herb or garlic garnish outweighs a plate of watery vegetablesHigher content per 100 g does not establish higher intake. Using the supplied article’s cilantro figure of 48 mg vitamin C per 100 g, a 5 g garnish supplies 2.4 mg. This illustrates serving arithmetic, not independent verification of that figure.
Watercress has almost twice the vitamin K of kaleA fixed ratio requires matched varieties, preparation states, and database entries. NIH’s cup-based figures also require corresponding weights before comparison per 100 g. [11]

Herbs count according to the amount eaten. Whole grains, other legumes, and fruit supply further fibre. A rotation of dark leafy greens, broccoli or peppers, and watery vegetables provides a simple shopping structure; colour alone cannot establish nutritional completeness. [1], [3]

Cooking: nutrient retention and oxalate reduction

  • Vitamin C retention:shorter steaming or microwaving can reduce some losses. Prolonged soaking, boiling, and hot holding can deplete water-soluble vitamins. [6]
  • Soluble oxalate reduction:boiling and discarding the water removes some oxalate but also some water-soluble nutrients. Experimental reductions vary by vegetable and treatment; a brief blanch does not have a universal effect or make shepherd’s purse equivalent to milk. [8]
  • Watercress:contaminated raw aquatic plants can transmit Fasciola. Wild plants from unknown waterways are not ready-to-eat salad ingredients. [9]
